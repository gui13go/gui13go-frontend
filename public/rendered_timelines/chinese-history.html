Chinese History: Five Millennia of Dynasties & Transformation

From the legendary Xia and oracle bones of the Shang to imperial unifications, the Silk Road, revolutionary upheavals, and modern resurgence.

c. 2070 BCE

The Xia Dynasty & Flood Control of Yu the Great

+

According to Chinese historical tradition and excavations at the Erlitou archaeological site in Henan, Yu the Great founded China's first hereditary dynasty. Confronting the catastrophic inundations of the Yellow River basin, Yu abandoned his father Gun's failed damming strategy in favor of extensive dredging, canalization, and drainage toward the sea. By organizing mass communal labor across disparate clans, Yu established centralized authority, and the succession of his son Qi inaugurated the dynastic hereditary model (jiatianxia) replacing merit-based abdication.

“Yu was so dedicated to dredging the waterways that in thirteen years he passed his own home three times without entering.”

— Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji)
c. 1600 BCE

The Shang Dynasty, Piece-Mold Bronzes & Oracle Bone Script

+

Centering its power along the Yellow River plain with monumental capitals at Zhengzhou and Yin (Anyang), the Shang dynasty established China's earliest historically and archaeologically confirmed state. Shang society achieved extraordinary mastery of piece-mold bronze metallurgy, casting massive ritual vessels (Ding) for ancestral banquets. Crucially, divination priests incised pyromantic questions onto turtle plastrons and ox scapulae (Jiaguwen), creating the earliest corpus of Chinese characters—logographic writing directly ancestral to modern Chinese.

“The great affairs of a state are sacrifice and war.”

— Zuo Zhuan (Commentary of Zuo)
1046 BCE

The Zhou Dynasty & The Mandate of Heaven (Tianming)

+

King Wu of Zhou led a coalition of allied tribes to overthrow the Shang tyrant King Zhou at the decisive Battle of Muye. Confronting the challenge of legitimizing their rule to Shang subjects, King Wu's brother, the Duke of Zhou (Dan), articulated the foundational constitutional philosophy of Chinese civilization: Tianming (The Mandate of Heaven). Heaven (Tian) grants the supreme right to rule (Son of Heaven / Tianzi) purely on moral rectitude and benevolent governance; when a ruler becomes corrupt and oppressive, Heaven revokes the mandate, legitimizing righteous rebellion.

“Heaven sees as my people see; Heaven hears as my people hear.”

— Book of Documents (Shangshu)
c. 770 BCE

Spring and Autumn Period & Confucius (Kong Fuzi)

+

Following the sack of Haojing by Quanrong nomads in 771 BCE, King Ping moved the Zhou capital eastward to Luoyang, commencing the Eastern Zhou. As central royal authority decayed into hundreds of warring feudal fiefdoms, Chinese philosophy experienced its greatest intellectual explosion: the Hundred Schools of Thought. Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551–479 BCE) travelled across the feudal states teaching that societal harmony is restored through moral self-cultivation, filial piety (Xiao), ritual propriety (Li), and empathetic benevolence (Ren).

“Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire. Guide them by virtue, regulate them by ritual, and the people will have a sense of shame and reform themselves.”

— Confucius, The Analects (Lunyu)
c. 500 BCE

Laozi and the Philosophy of the Daodejing

+

Contrasting with Confucian emphasis on social hierarchy and ritual conduct, the enigmatic sage Laozi composed the Daodejing, giving rise to Daoism. Laozi posited the Dao (The Way) as the ineffable, generative cosmic source underlying all existence. Daoist thought champions Wu Wei (effortless, spontaneous non-action), natural simplicity (Ziran), humility, and yielding like water. Daoism became the philosophical and spiritual complement to Confucianism, deeply shaping Chinese landscape painting, medicine, poetry, and martial arts.

“The Dao that can be spoken of is not the eternal Dao. The name that can be named is not the eternal name. The softest things in the world overcome the hardest.”

— Laozi, Daodejing
c. 475 BCE

Warring States Period & Sun Tzu's Art of War

+

The geopolitical landscape consolidated into seven formidable powers—Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Han, Wei, and Zhao—waging total interstate war with mass peasant conscription, cast-iron weaponry, mounted archers, and high-tensile crossbows. In this era of relentless military doctrine, general and strategist Sun Wu formulated The Art of War (Sunzi Bingfa). Sun Tzu elevated warfare into a comprehensive science of psychology, geography, deception, economic sustainability, and swift maneuvers.

“The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting. If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.”

— Sun Tzu, The Art of War (Sunzi Bingfa)
221 BCE

Qin Shi Huang Unifies China: The First Imperial State

+

Through rigorous Legalist statecraft, agricultural colonization, and administrative discipline orchestrated by reformer Shang Yang and Prime Minister Li Si, King Ying Zheng of Qin systematically conquered all six rival kingdoms, unifying China for the first time. Assuming the divine title Qin Shi Huang (First Sovereign Emperor), he abolished hereditary feudalism, divided the empire into 36 centralized commanderies, connected northern frontier fortifications into the Great Wall, and standardized Chinese script (Small Seal), axle widths, copper coinage, and weights and measures.

“All under heaven is unified; weapons are collected and melted into giant bells; laws and measures are made uniform across the four corners of the realm.”

— Qin Imperial Inscription on Mount Tai (219 BCE)
202 BCE

The Han Dynasty: Peasant Founder Liu Bang & Imperial Synthesis

+

Following Qin Shi Huang's death, tyrannical excesses sparked widespread peasant revolts. In the ensuing Chu-Han Contention, peasant-born constable Liu Bang outmaneuvered aristocratic hegemon Xiang Yu at the Battle of Gaixia, founding the Han dynasty as Emperor Gaozu. The Han synthesized Qin's efficient administrative bureaucracy with Confucian moral statecraft, lowered tax burdens, promoted economic recovery, and established the foundational imperial identity that led the majority ethnic population of China to identify as the 'Han people'.

“A great wind rises, clouds fly high. Having commanded all within the seas, I return to my native land. Where shall I find brave warriors to guard the four quarters?”

— Emperor Gaozu (Liu Bang), Song of the Great Wind (Dafeng Ge)
138 BCE

Zhang Qian's Mission to the Western Regions & The Silk Road

+

Seeking military alliances against the predatory Xiongnu nomadic confederation, Emperor Wu of Han dispatched court envoy Zhang Qian on a perilous expedition across the Tarim Basin. Though held captive by the Xiongnu for over a decade, Zhang Qian successfully reached the Greco-Bactrian and Sogdian civilizations of Central Asia (Dayuan, Kangju, Daxia). His intelligence reports prompted Han military expansion into the Hexi Corridor, opening the trans-continental Silk Road that connected Chang'an to Rome and Persia for trade in silk, horses, glass, and Buddhist philosophy.

“He opened the path to the Western Regions, bringing back knowledge of heavenly horses, alfalfa, grapes, and the great realms of the West.”

— Ban Gu, Book of Han (Hanshu)
c. 91 BCE

Sima Qian Completes the Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji)

+

When Grand Historian Sima Qian defended defeated general Li Ling, Emperor Wu sentenced him to choose between execution and the ultimate humiliation of castration. Sima Qian chose castration so he could fulfill his filial pledge to finish the Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian). Covering 2,500 years of civilization from the Yellow Emperor down to Emperor Wu through basic annals, chronological tables, treatises, and hereditary biographies, the Shiji invented modern Chinese historiography with rigorous objectivity and literary brilliance.

“Though death befalls all men alike, it may be weightier than Mount Tai or lighter than a swan feather. I had to complete this work before my life could end.”

— Sima Qian, Letter to Ren An (Bao Ren An Shu)
105 CE

Cai Lun Perfects the Manufacture of Paper

+

Eastern Han court official Cai Lun presented Emperor He with a standardized industrial method for manufacturing high-quality writing paper using mulberry bark, hemp waste, tattered cloth, and fishnets. Prior to Cai Lun, texts were inscribed on heavy, cumbersome bamboo slips or costly silk rolls. Cai Lun's cheap, lightweight, and durable paper triggered an educational, administrative, and literary revolution across China before transmitting along the Silk Road to the Islamic world and Europe.

“Cai Lun devised the process of making paper from the bark of trees, remnants of hemp, rags of cloth, and fishing nets. The Emperor commended his skill, and henceforth it was universally adopted.”

— Fan Ye, Book of the Later Han (Hou Hanshu)
220 CE

Fall of the Han & The Three Kingdoms Era (Wei, Shu, Wu)

+

Following the millenarian Yellow Turban Rebellion and warlord factionalism, Cao Cao consolidated the northern plains, culminating in his naval defeat at the pivotal Battle of Red Cliffs (208 CE). In 220 CE, Cao Pi forced the abdication of Emperor Xian of Han, officially partitioning China into three warring states: Cao Wei in the central plains, Shu Han under imperial kin Liu Bei and strategist Zhuge Liang in Sichuan, and Eastern Wu under Sun Quan south of the Yangtze. The era produced timeless lessons in military tactics, diplomacy, and loyalty.

“The empire, long divided, must unite; long united, must divide. Thus it has ever been.”

— Luo Guanzhong, Romance of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguo Yanyi)
581 CE

The Sui Dynasty & Construction of the Grand Canal

+

Following three and a half centuries of political fragmentation during the Northern and Southern Dynasties, Emperor Wen (Yang Jian) reunited northern and southern China, founding the Sui dynasty. His successor, Emperor Yang, conscripted millions of laborers to construct the Grand Canal (Da Yunhe)—an engineering triumph extending over 1,700 kilometers connecting the agricultural breadbasket of the Yangtze river delta (Hangzhou) to the political and military capitals of Luoyang and Beijing.

“The Grand Canal united the lifeblood of the empire, transporting southern grain to supply northern garrisons and weaving the realm into a single economic fabric.”

— Wei Zheng, Book of Sui (Suishu)
618 CE

The Tang Dynasty: Cosmopolitan Golden Age & Classical Poetry

+

Founded by Li Yuan and elevated by his son Emperor Taizong (Li Shimin) during the Zhenguan reign, the Tang dynasty marked a zenith of Chinese power, cosmopolitan culture, and artistic expression. Its capital, Chang'an, grew into the world's largest metropolis with over one million inhabitants within its grid-planned walls, welcoming Persian merchants, Nestorian Christian monks, and Japanese embassies. Tang culture witnessed the apex of classical poetry through Li Bai and Du Fu, and early woodblock printing produced the 868 CE Diamond Sutra.

“The moon beams shine before my bed; I wonder if it could be frost on the ground. Looking up, I gaze at the luminous moon; looking down, I yearn for my distant home.”

— Li Bai, Quiet Night Thought (Jing Ye Si)
690 CE

Wu Zetian Proclaims the Zhou Dynasty: China's Sole Female Emperor

+

Rising from imperial concubine to Empress consort of Gaozong, Wu Zetian seized the imperial throne in her own right, proclaiming the Second Zhou Dynasty and reigning as Huangdi (Emperor). Wu Zetian consolidated power by reforming the imperial civil service examinations (Keju), evaluating officials on merit rather than hereditary aristocratic pedigree. She patronized Buddhism, oversaw the carving of the monumental Vairocana Buddha at Longmen Grottoes, and maintained external military hegemony throughout Central Asia.

“Rule the realm through righteousness and talent, not through birth and factional pedigree. Let the wise govern and the people prosper.”

— Imperial Edicts of Empress Wu Zetian
755 CE

The An Lushan Rebellion: Cataclysmic Turning Point of the Tang

+

General An Lushan, military commissioner (Jiedushi) of three northern border garrisons, rebelled against Emperor Xuanzong and captured both eastern and western capitals (Luoyang and Chang'an). The catastrophic eight-year war devastated northern China's demographic and tax base, caused tens of millions of casualties and refugees, and permanently shifted China's economic and population center south to the Yangtze basin while weakening central Tang imperial authority.

“The nation is broken, but mountains and rivers remain. The city turns spring, grass and trees grow thick. Moved by the times, flowers draw tears; hating separation, birds shock the heart.”

— Du Fu, Spring View (Chun Wang)
960 CE

The Song Dynasty: Pre-Modern Commercial & Technological Zenith

+

Emperor Taizu (Zhao Kuangyin) unified China and instituted civilian bureaucratic control over military commanders. The Song dynasty witnessed an unprecedented commercial revolution: the world's first government-issued paper currency (Jiaozi), the mariner's magnetic compass for oceanic voyages, movable-type ceramic printing by Bi Sheng, and the deployment of gunpowder bombs and fire lances in siege warfare.

“Trade flourished beyond the city walls, night markets burned till dawn, and paper money circulated from Sichuan to the sea in great prosperity.”

— Meng Yuanlao, The Eastern Capital: A Dream of Splendor (Dongjing Meng Hua Lu)
1127 CE

The Jingkang Incident & The Southern Song Renaissance

+

Jurchen cavalry of the Jin dynasty sacked the northern Song capital of Kaifeng (the Jingkang Incident), capturing Emperor Qinzong and retired Emperor Huizong. The imperial court retreated south of the Yangtze River to Lin'an (Hangzhou), founding the Southern Song. Despite northern territorial losses, Southern Song Hangzhou became the wealthiest urban metropolis in the world, marked by flourishing maritime trade, landscape painting, and the codification of Neo-Confucian philosophy by master Zhu Xi.

“My wrath bristles through my helmet! ... Thirty years of deeds and dust, eight thousand leagues of cloud and moon. Do not wait in vain, or youth's hair turns white with regret!”

— General Yue Fei, The River Runs Red (Man Jiang Hong)
1271 CE

Kublai Khan Founds the Yuan Dynasty & The Pax Mongolica

+

Grandson of Genghis Khan, Kublai Khan conquered the Southern Song in 1279, completing the Mongol conquest of all China. Adopting the Chinese dynastic title 'Da Yuan' (Great Prime) and moving his capital to Dadu/Khanbaliq (modern Beijing), he integrated China into the vast Eurasian Mongol trade network (Pax Mongolica), welcoming Venetian merchant Marco Polo and Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta.

“The Great Khan's palace in Cambaluc is the largest, most sumptuous, and most marvelous that ever was seen by mortal eyes.”

— The Travels of Marco Polo
1368 CE

The Ming Dynasty: Peasant Rebel Zhu Yuanzhang Restores Han Rule

+

Rising from an impoverished peasant and Buddhist monk orphaned by plague, Zhu Yuanzhang joined the Red Turban rebellion, drove the Mongol Yuan court out of Beijing back into the northern steppes, and declared the Ming dynasty as the Hongwu Emperor in Nanjing. He reconstructed the agrarian tax base (Fish Scale Registers), restored civil service exams, and rebuilt stone border defenses into the iconic Ming Great Wall.

“Drive out the foreign barbarians, restore China, bring order to the land, and establish lasting peace for the common people.”

— Hongwu Emperor's Restoration Proclamation (1368)
1405 CE

Admiral Zheng He's Seven Oceanic Treasure Voyages

+

Commissioned by the Yongle Emperor, Hui Muslim eunuch Admiral Zheng He led seven epic naval expeditions spanning from 1405 to 1433 across the Indian Ocean to Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, and the Swahili coast of East Africa. Commanding fleets of hundreds of vessels—including colossal nine-masted treasure ships (Baochuan)—Zheng He projected Chinese majesty, diplomacy, and trade.

“We have traversed more than one hundred thousand li of immense waters and have beheld in the ocean huge waves like mountains rising up to the heavens, sailing with billowing sails cloud-like day and night.”

— Zheng He, Changle Stele Inscription (1431)
1420 CE

Construction of the Forbidden City & Imperial Beijing

+

The Yongle Emperor moved the primary imperial capital north to Beijing and completed the Forbidden City (Zijin Cheng). Spanning 72 hectares with 980 surviving buildings, this monumental complex of timber, golden-glazed tiles, and marble terracing served as the ceremonial and administrative heart of the Ming and Qing empires for five centuries.

“Constructed along the sacred north-south meridian, the palace mirrors the celestial court of the North Star, radiating harmony to all under heaven.”

— Ming Imperial Architectural Records
1644 CE

The Qing Dynasty: Manchu Conquest Through the Shanhai Pass

+

As peasant rebel Li Zicheng sacked Beijing and the last Ming emperor hanged himself on Jingshan Hill, Ming general Wu Sangui opened the strategic Shanhai Pass of the Great Wall. The Manchu Eight Banners swept south, taking Beijing and establishing the Qing dynasty under Emperor Shunzhi, uniting Manchuria, Mongolia, and the Han heartland under Manchu rule.

“Banners united, customs respected, the mandate passes to the Great Qing to restore order to the realm.”

— Regent Dorgon's Edict to the People of Beijing
1661–1796 CE

The High Qing Era: Kangxi, Yongzheng & Qianlong

+

A 135-year era of profound stability, demographic growth, and territorial expansion. The Kangxi Emperor crushed the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, compiled the authoritative Kangxi Dictionary, and integrated Taiwan; the Qianlong Emperor incorporated Xinjiang, Tibet, and Mongolia, expanding China's borders to over 13 million square kilometers while commissioning the Siku Quanshu encyclopedia.

“We possess all things in prolific abundance and desire not your country's manufactures. There is not the slightest need to import the products of outside barbarians.”

— Qianlong Emperor's Letter to King George III of England (1793)
1839 CE

First Opium War & Beginning of the Century of Humiliation

+

Imperial Commissioner Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed 20,000 chests of illegal British opium in Humen, Guangdong. The British Empire dispatched ironclad steam gunboats (Nemesis) to blockade ports and bombard coastal defenses. Defeated, the Qing signed the 1842 Treaty of Nanking—the first 'Unequal Treaty'—ceding Hong Kong Island, paying massive indemnities, and opening five treaty ports to foreign extraterritoriality.

“By what right do British merchants use this poisonous drug to injure the Chinese people? Even in your own country it is forbidden to smoke it.”

— Commissioner Lin Zexu, Memorial to Queen Victoria (1839)
1850 CE

The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom: Total Civil War

+

Hong Xiuquan, influenced by Christian missionary tracts and believing himself Jesus Christ's younger brother, launched the Taiping Rebellion against the 'Manchu demons'. Establishing their capital at Tianjing (Nanjing), the Taipings instituted radical land redistribution, gender equality, and communal property. The 14-year war claimed an estimated 20 to 30 million lives before Zeng Guofan's regional Xiang Army crushed the revolt.

“Where there is land, let all cultivate it together; where there is rice, let all eat it together; where there are clothes, let all wear them together.”

— Taiping Heavenly Kingdom Agrarian Charter
1894 CE

First Sino-Japanese War & Treaty of Shimonoseki

+

Clashing over sphere of influence in Korea, modernizing Meiji Japan decisively defeated the Qing's flagship Beiyang Fleet at the Battle of the Yalu River and captured Weihaiwei. The resulting 1894–1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki forced the cession of Taiwan and the Pescadores to Japan and triggered the 'Scramble for Concessions' among Western powers carving China into spheres of influence.

“A single blow shattered the illusion of our military self-strengthening; the island nation has humbled the Middle Kingdom.”

— Li Hongzhang, Post-War Memorial
1900 CE

The Boxer Uprising & The Eight-Nation Alliance

+

The Righteous and Harmonious Fists (Boxers)—a martial arts movement protesting foreign missionary encroachment, drought, and imperialist railways—besieged the foreign diplomatic legations in Beijing with the tacit blessing of Empress Dowager Cixi. An Eight-Nation Alliance (Britain, Russia, Japan, France, US, Germany, Italy, Austria-Hungary) invaded, looted Beijing, and imposed the punitive 1901 Boxer Protocol indemnity.

“Support the Qing, exterminate the foreigners! Drive out the invaders who carve our land like a melon.”

— Boxer Slogan (Yihetuan Placard, 1900)
1911 CE

The Xinhai Revolution & End of Imperial China

+

On October 10, 1911, an accidental bomb detonation in Hankou sparked the Wuchang Uprising among modern army soldiers affiliated with Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary alliance (Tongmenghui). Province after province declared secession from the Qing. On February 12, 1912, child emperor Puyi abdicated the dragon throne, terminating 2,132 years of imperial dynastic monarchy.

“The imperial dynasty has abdicated. China now belongs to the people as a republic, founded on nationalism, democracy, and the people's livelihood.”

— Sun Yat-sen, Inauguration as Provisional President (January 1912)
1919 CE

The May Fourth Movement: Cultural & Intellectual Awakening

+

Over 3,000 students from Peking University marched to Tiananmen protesting the Paris Peace Conference decision to award Germany's colonial concessions in Shandong to Japan rather than return them to China. The protest expanded into a nationwide cultural renaissance (New Culture Movement) championed by Chen Duxiu, Lu Xun, and Hu Shi, advocating vernacular Chinese (Baihua), democracy ('Mr. De'), and science ('Mr. Sai').

“Save the nation from subjugation! Boycott Japanese goods! Out with the national traitors!”

— May Fourth Student Manifesto (1919)
1937–1945 CE

Second Sino-Japanese War & China in World War II

+

Following the Marco Polo Bridge skirmish on July 7, 1937, Imperial Japan launched a full-scale invasion of China, committing horrific atrocities during the Nanjing Massacre (December 1937). Despite staggering devastation and relocating the wartime capital inland to Chongqing, Chinese forces under the Second United Front tied down over one million Japanese soldiers for eight brutal years, making China an indispensable Allied victor in WWII.

“If war begins, there is no distinction between north and south, young and old; everyone has the duty to resist and defend the homeland.”

— Chiang Kai-shek, Lushan Declaration (July 1937)
1949 CE

Proclamation of the People's Republic of China

+

Following the victory of the People's Liberation Army over the Nationalist Kuomintang forces in the Chinese Civil War, Mao Zedong stood atop the Tiananmen Gate in Beijing on October 1, 1949, to officially proclaim the founding of the People's Republic of China, bringing national unification after more than a century of foreign invasion, warlordism, and civil strife.

“The Chinese people, comprising one quarter of humanity, have now stood up! Our nation will never again be an insulted nation.”

— Mao Zedong, Address at the First CPPCC (September 1949)
1978 CE

Deng Xiaoping & Reform and Opening Up (Gaige Kaifang)

+

At the historic Third Plenum of the 11th CPC Central Committee in December 1978, Deng Xiaoping shifted national focus to modernization and market mechanisms under 'Socialism with Chinese Characteristics'. The policies dismantled agrarian communes (Household Responsibility System), authorized private enterprise, and established Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Xiamen), unleashing four decades of world-historic economic growth.

“It doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice. Emancipate the mind, seek truth from facts, and unite as one in looking to the future.”

— Deng Xiaoping, Speech at the Central Work Conference (December 1978)
1997 CE

The Return of Hong Kong to Chinese Sovereignty

+

At the stroke of midnight on July 1, 1997, after 156 years of British colonial administration dating back to the First Opium War and the 1898 99-year lease on the New Territories, the British Union Jack and Hong Kong colonial ensign were lowered, and the five-starred red flag of China alongside the newly created bauhinia regional flag were raised at the Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre. The historic handover established Hong Kong as a Special Administrative Region (SAR) governed under Deng Xiaoping's constitutional principle of 'One Country, Two Systems', guaranteeing high autonomy, independent legal and judicial systems, and capitalist economic continuity.

“The return of Hong Kong to the motherland marks an end to the historical legacy of the past, wipes out a century-old national humiliation, and opens a new era of national rejuvenation under One Country, Two Systems.”

— President Jiang Zemin, Handover Ceremony Address (July 1, 1997)
1999 CE

The Return of Macau to Chinese Sovereignty

+

At midnight on December 20, 1999, the Portuguese national flag was lowered and the flag of the People's Republic of China together with the green lotus flag of the Macau Special Administrative Region were hoisted at the Macau Cultural Centre. Portuguese settlement in Macau dated back to 1557 under the Ming dynasty, making it the first and final European-administered territory in East Asia. The peaceful transfer of Macau after 442 years of Portuguese administration officially concluded European colonial presence on Chinese soil, inaugurating Macau's rapid economic transformation into a global tourism and leisure hub under 'One Country, Two Systems'.

“The return of Macau marks another major milestone in the great cause of the reunification of the Chinese nation. From this moment on, Macau enters a completely new era.”

— President Jiang Zemin, Macau Handover Ceremony Address (December 20, 1999)
2001–Present

WTO Accession, Space Exploration & 21st-Century Superpower

+

On December 11, 2001, China officially joined the World Trade Organization, supercharging its integration into global supply chains and becoming the 'factory of the world'. Over the subsequent two decades, China lifted more than 800 million citizens out of poverty, built the world's longest high-speed rail network (45,000+ km), hosted the 2008 and 2022 Olympic Games, launched the Belt and Road Initiative, and constructed the Tiangong space station alongside lunar exploration.

“The Chinese Dream is the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation. We must unswervingly follow the path of peaceful development and mutual benefit.”

— National Address, Beijing
